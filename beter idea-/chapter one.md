# Chapter One: The Last Command

## Scene I — Micro, Macro, and the Cost of One More Turn

The battlefield was collapsing.

Mike saw it three minutes before it happened—not because of a flashing alert or some dramatic red warning banner, but because of the way the map _felt_ when he zoomed all the way out.

At first glance, everything looked fine.

His front lines held. Cities still flew his faction’s banners. Casualty numbers were acceptable—better than expected, even. Anyone watching the match through the public spectator client would’ve said he was winning on points, tempo, maybe even morale.

But Mike wasn’t looking at points.

He was looking at flow.

He toggled off unit models, stripped the display down to logistics overlays and command latency. The battlefield transformed from a mess of icons and explosions into something cleaner—lines, vectors, pressure zones.

That’s where he saw it.

Red bled in at the edges of the minimap. Not sudden. Not dramatic. Just a slow, invasive discoloration, like mold spreading beneath paint. Supply convoys delayed by seconds here, then minutes there. Communication pings taking just a little too long to resolve. A battalion hesitating at a crossroads—not enough to trigger an alert, but enough to matter.

The AI opponent hadn’t made a single flashy move.

It didn’t need to.

It was strangling him.

“Yeah,” Mike muttered, leaning closer to the screen. “There it is.”

He dragged the camera east, then south, fingers moving with practiced ease. Windows opened and closed in rapid succession. To an observer, it would look like panic—random clicks, half-formed commands, indecision.

It wasn’t.

Mike rerouted a reserve battalion away from a besieged city, redirecting it instead to a barren stretch of terrain twelve kilometers behind the lines. He ordered an engineering unit to abandon a nearly completed fortification. He canceled an air strike mid-flight, letting enemy armor roll unchallenged through an open valley.

The chat exploded instantly.

> **WHAT ARE YOU DOING??**

> **THROWING??**

> **HE HAD THIS WTF**

Mike didn’t look at the chat window. He never did once things reached this stage.

The AI adjusted, of course. It always did. Enemy icons shifted, recalculating, testing. Probes pushed into the gaps he’d opened, confident, efficient.

Mike let them.

He sacrificed air superiority—not because he couldn’t contest it, but because the fuel expenditure curve had just crossed a threshold he didn’t like. He pulled elite units back _early_, before they were under pressure, before their withdrawal would look justified.

The jeers got louder.

> **WHY ARE YOU RUNNING??**

Mike exhaled slowly through his nose.

Running wasn’t losing.

Running was buying time.

He split his attention now—half on the macro map, half on the invisible clock ticking behind it. Every action had a cost measured not just in resources, but in _attention_. The longer this went on, the more human error would creep in. The AI didn’t get tired. It didn’t get distracted by chat, or by the ache in the shoulders, or by the subtle burn behind the eyes that came from staring at a screen too long.

Mike did.

He issued a flurry of low-level commands—adjusting rally points, tweaking formation priorities, setting conditional orders that would only trigger if certain thresholds were crossed. None of it would show up on a highlight reel. None of it would earn praise.

But it slowed the bleed.

Barely.

The minimap stabilized, red zones halting their advance like water hitting a dam. For a moment—just a moment—the battlefield reached a fragile equilibrium.

Mike leaned back slightly, rolling his shoulders. His heart was beating faster than he liked. Not panic. Just exertion. The kind that came from sustained focus, from holding too many variables in your head at once.

He glanced at the clock in the corner of the screen.

Two minutes, forty-one seconds.

Close.

Too close.

“One more adjustment,” he murmured.

He zoomed in on a contested region near the center of the map—a logistical hub the AI had been probing for the last five turns. Mike had left it intentionally under-defended, feeding the illusion of weakness. Now he snapped the trap shut.

A delayed artillery barrage activated. Hidden reserves surged forward. The enemy advance faltered, momentum breaking like a wave against rock.

Chat erupted again, tone shifting from outrage to confusion.

> **WAIT**

> **HOW DID HE—**

Mike didn’t smile.

This wasn’t a win. It was a _pause_.

The AI recalculated almost instantly. New vectors appeared, skirting the edges of the engagement, already looking for the next point of failure. Mike followed them, eyes darting, mind racing ahead, projecting outcomes five, six, seven moves deep.

The counteroffensive was forming in his head now—a beautiful, brutal sequence that would collapse the AI’s extended supply network and force it into a defensive posture for the first time all match.

He just needed—

A sharp pain lanced through his left arm.

Mike froze.

The mouse slipped slightly under his fingers, cursor drifting off-target. For a fraction of a second, he thought he’d just leaned awkwardly, pinched a nerve.

Then the pain intensified.

“Oh,” he said, voice calm, almost curious. “That’s… not ideal.”

His chest tightened. Not like a punch—more like a vise slowly cranking shut. Breath came harder, shallower. The room seemed to tilt, just a degree or two, enough to throw off his sense of balance.

On the screen, units waited patiently for orders.

They always did.

Mike tried to move his hand back into position. It responded sluggishly, fingers numb, uncooperative. His heart hammered now, loud enough that he could hear it over the hum of the PC fans.

Two minutes, twelve seconds.

“So,” he muttered, forcing air into his lungs. “We’re doing this now.”

Another spike of pain flared, radiating outward, down his arm, into his jaw. His vision tunneled slightly at the edges, colors dulling.

He leaned back in his chair, not because he wanted to, but because his body decided for him.

The battlefield continued to evolve without his input. Conditional orders triggered. Reserves moved. The trap he’d set earlier finished closing, enemy units bogged down in a kill zone that would, under normal circumstances, have turned the tide completely.

Mike watched it happen with detached awareness.

Good execution, he thought distantly.

His breathing hitched. Sweat beaded along his hairline, trickling down his temple. The pain was constant now, no longer sharp but crushing, relentless.

He laughed once, quietly. A short, breathless sound.

“Figures,” he said.

His gaze flicked to the minimap one last time. The red zones were shrinking. The counteroffensive was _almost_ online. Another thirty seconds, maybe less, and the AI would be forced to respond on his terms.

He reached for the mouse again.

His hand missed.

The room spun.

As his chair tipped and the floor rushed up to meet him, Mike’s final thought wasn’t fear, or regret, or the sudden realization of his own mortality.

It was irritation.

_Damn it. I almost had the counteroffensive online._

The clock kept ticking.

## Scene II — Defeat Condition: Biological

Mike didn’t feel himself hit the floor.

There was no sharp impact, no cinematic crash. Just a sudden, disorienting absence of support, followed by pressure in places pressure didn’t belong. His cheek pressed against cold laminate. One arm was twisted beneath him at an awkward angle. The other lay limp at his side, fingers still faintly curled as if expecting a mouse that was no longer there.

The pain in his chest hadn’t lessened.

If anything, it had become more diffuse—less a spike, more a total occupation. Every breath was work now. Not impossible, just… inefficient. Like trying to run an optimized program on failing hardware.

Okay, he thought distantly. That’s bad.

The thought arrived without panic. Panic required energy, and his system was already reallocating resources whether he liked it or not.

The PC monitor loomed above him, tilted at a slight angle. From the floor, the battlefield looked different—less commanding, more detached. Units moved. Explosions bloomed and faded. Status indicators updated in real time.

The game hadn’t paused.

Of course it hadn’t.

Mike focused on breathing. In. Out. Short, shallow cycles that barely seemed to accomplish anything. His heart was racing now, each beat a heavy, uneven thud that echoed in his ears.

There was a tightness in his left arm, numbness creeping down toward his fingers. His jaw felt strange too—stiff, unresponsive, like it belonged to someone else.

Classic, some distant, analytical part of his mind supplied. Textbook, even.

He wondered vaguely why he hadn’t noticed the warning signs earlier. Fatigue, maybe. The dull ache he’d been ignoring for weeks. The way stairs had started feeling steeper than they used to.

Should’ve scheduled a checkup.

Another breath. Another wave of pressure.

His vision flickered, dimming at the edges. The ceiling above him—white, cracked in one corner where a previous tenant had tried and failed to fix a leak—blurred in and out of focus.

Somewhere, an alarm chimed.

Not the game. The apartment.

A soft, insistent beeping from his phone on the desk. A reminder he’d set and forgotten about.

Hydration break, it probably said.

Mike almost laughed.

His fingers twitched, trying to respond to a command that never came. His body wasn’t listening anymore. Input lag was climbing fast.

On-screen, the AI made its move.

A feint on the western front. A probing strike that would have been laughable under normal circumstances. Mike had already calculated the response. He could see it clearly—shift the artillery, rotate reserves, force a retreat.

His lips parted.

No sound came out.

That’s new, he thought, with mild surprise.

The pressure in his chest spiked again, sharper this time, and his breathing stuttered. Each inhale felt like it stopped halfway, caught on something that refused to move.

Okay. That’s worse.

His thoughts began to fragment, slipping out of sequence. He tried to pull them back together, to prioritize, to _do something_, but the mental clarity he relied on was dissolving.

The hum of the PC fans seemed louder now. Or maybe everything else was getting quieter.

The city outside his window carried on without him—distant traffic, a muffled siren somewhere far away, the faint thrum of life continuing at its usual pace.

On-screen, his forces stabilized.

The trap he’d set earlier finished closing. Enemy units stalled, then began to withdraw. The minimap shifted, red zones receding as supply lines collapsed under sustained pressure.

If he’d still been in his chair, he would’ve capitalized on it.

If.

The thought felt oddly hollow.

His gaze drifted, unfocused, back to the monitor. The UI elements blurred together now, text smearing at the edges. He blinked, slowly, deliberately, trying to clear it.

It didn’t help.

He became aware, dimly, of how cold the floor was beneath him. How heavy his limbs felt. How distant everything seemed, like he was observing the scene through layers of glass.

This is it, then, he thought.

The realization didn’t come with a spike of fear or a rush of emotion. It settled in quietly, like a status update.

Condition critical.

No recovery options available.

He wondered, briefly, if anyone would find him quickly. The building manager, maybe. Or a neighbor complaining about noise. He hadn’t exactly been social.

Another alarm chimed. Louder this time.

His phone, vibrating against the desk.

He couldn’t turn his head to look at it.

The game clock ticked on, indifferent.

Mike’s breathing became erratic, shallow gasps that barely pulled in air. His heart hammered, then stuttered, then hammered again, rhythm slipping out of sync.

His thoughts slowed.

He tried to review his life, out of habit more than nostalgia. It was something people talked about, right? A montage. Regrets. Highlights.

Nothing came.

Just fragments. Matches played. Strategies refined. Long nights chasing perfect execution. The quiet satisfaction of seeing a plan unfold exactly as predicted.

No family faces. No dramatic confessions.

He didn’t feel cheated.

Just… annoyed.

So close, he thought.

The counteroffensive had been _right there_. Another minute—less, even—and he would’ve forced the AI into a defensive spiral it couldn’t recover from.

A soft, distant sound reached his ears, like rushing water. Or maybe static. His vision dimmed further, colors draining until the world looked washed out, unreal.

His chest hurt less now.

That worried him.

His heart gave one heavy, uneven beat.

Then another.

Then—nothing.

The hum of the PC fans continued.

The battlefield reached equilibrium.

Units followed their last orders perfectly, executing commands issued by a player who was no longer there to see the result.

The defeat screen never appeared.

The game clock kept ticking, counting time for someone who had just run out of it.

## Scene III — Black Screen, No UI

There was darkness.

Not the comfortable kind. Not sleep, or rest, or even unconsciousness. This darkness had texture to it—dense, heavy, pressing in from all sides. It wasn’t empty. It was _full_ of absence.

Mike tried to open his eyes.

There was no response.

He tried to breathe.

That worked. Sort of. The sensation was wrong—automatic, distant, like the command had been routed through too many layers before reaching whatever passed for lungs now.

Okay, he thought. That’s… different.

The thought itself felt sluggish. Not slow, exactly, but compressed, like it had been packed into too small a space. Concepts arrived stripped of detail, edges blurred.

Something hummed.

Not sound, not quite. A vibration that existed somewhere between sensation and awareness. It pulsed once, paused, then pulsed again, each repetition a little clearer than the last.

> **—Initializing—**

The words weren’t heard. They were _impressed_, stamped directly onto whatever remained of his consciousness.

Mike tried to react.

He couldn’t tell if he succeeded.

> **—Error: Neural interface mismatch—**

That got his attention.

Interface implied connection. Mismatch implied assumptions—about structure, about compatibility. About expectations not being met.

> **—Recalibrating—**

The darkness shifted.

Pressure changed, easing in some places, increasing in others. Mike became dimly aware of boundaries—something enclosing him, constraining him, holding him together. He felt… small.

Compressed.

Like a program forced to run on insufficient hardware.

That’s bad design, he thought automatically.

The thought felt thin, but it was still _him_. That mattered.

> **—Warning: Cognitive bandwidth limited—**

Mike would have frowned if he’d had a face.

Limited bandwidth explained a lot. The fuzziness. The way thoughts arrived incomplete. He tried to push against it, to force clarity through sheer will.

Nothing happened.

Right. So brute force is out.

He let go.

The darkness persisted, but it no longer felt hostile. Just… there. A holding state. Somewhere between shutdown and reboot.

> **—Language package unavailable—**

That was new.

Language was foundational. Without it, meaning degraded fast. Mike felt the edges of his identity blur further, memories losing labels, concepts drifting without anchors.

He focused on something solid.

Strategy.

Systems.

Patterns.

Those held, faint but intact.

> **—Beginning adaptive transmission—**

The hum changed.

It simplified.

The impressions became rougher, less precise, but easier to parse. Concepts arrived not as words, but as _directives_.

> **…Observe…**

Observation he could do.

Even without eyes, there was input. Sensation, vague and undefined, but present. Pressure. Rhythm. Something pulsing nearby at a steady pace.

> **…Process…**

He did. Automatically. Categorizing, comparing, searching for reference points.

The pulsing felt familiar.

Heart? No. Too external.

> **…Improve…**

That one lingered.

Improve implied agency. Iteration. Growth over time.

Mike held onto that.

The darkness lightened, just a fraction. Or maybe his perception adjusted. It was hard to tell. Time itself felt unreliable, stretching and compressing without warning.

He tried to access memory.

His apartment surfaced first—dim, cluttered, the glow of the monitor. Then the battlefield. The minimap bleeding red. The pain.

The pain felt distant now, abstract. Logged, archived, no longer active.

He remembered falling.

He remembered irritation.

Then—nothing.

Dead, then, he concluded.

Or close enough that the distinction didn’t matter.

The system—whatever it was—continued its halting transmission, cycling through the same few concepts, adjusting them slightly each time, like an algorithm refining output based on feedback it barely understood.

> **…Observe. Process. Improve…**

Mike listened.

He didn’t know how long it went on. Without sensory anchors, time dissolved into a meaningless blur. There were no seconds, no minutes. Just repetition and gradual alignment.

The impressions sharpened.

Pressure became location. Rhythm became pattern. Warmth differentiated from cold.

He realized, slowly, that he wasn’t _floating_ in the darkness.

He was embedded in something.

Surrounded.

Held.

A new sensation intruded—constriction, followed by release. A squeezing motion, rhythmic and insistent, compressing whatever form he currently occupied.

Unpleasant.

The system paused.

> **—Reassessing—**

The squeezing intensified.

Mike tried to react, to brace, but his concept of a body was theoretical at best. He experienced the sensation without context, a raw data stream with no labels.

Then—

Light.

Not a gentle fade-in. An explosion. A flood of input so overwhelming it felt like his consciousness might tear apart under the strain.

Sound crashed in alongside it—chaotic, overlapping, harsh. Sharp noises, loud and close, reverberating through him in ways that made no sense.

His awareness spiked.

Too much. Too fast.

> **—Sensory overload detected—**

No kidding.

Something squeezed him again, harder this time, and panic—real, instinctive panic—flared for the first time since the darkness.

Air rushed into him.

Burned.

Mike screamed.

The sound tore out of him, high-pitched and raw, vibrating through his entire being. It shocked him—not just because of the volume, but because of the _quality_.

Weak.

Thin.

Pathetic.

That sound came from _him_.

> **…Breathing achieved…**

The system sounded almost… relieved.

> **…Motor function limited… Acceptable…**

Hands touched him.

Large hands. Warm. Careful. They supported him, adjusted his position, wiped something wet from his skin. The contact grounded him in a way the abstract darkness never had.

He felt gravity.

Felt weight.

Felt himself being lifted.

Voices surrounded him, rising and falling with emotional intensity he couldn’t parse yet. The sounds had structure—patterns that repeated, tones that suggested meaning—but he didn’t understand a single word.

Language barrier, he realized dimly.

The system tried again, its transmission simplifying further, abandoning precision in favor of approximation.

> **…Safe…**

> **…High-resource environment…**

> **…Survivability: Optimal…**

Mike clung to that last one.

Optimal was good.

The world was too loud, too bright, too _much_, but the panic ebbed as his body—his _new_ body—settled into a fragile equilibrium.

He couldn’t move properly. His limbs responded sluggishly, uncoordinated. His vision was a blur of shapes and light. His hearing was sharp to the point of pain.

But he was alive.

Rebooted.

Reallocated.

He didn’t know where he was.

Didn’t know what he was.

Didn’t know what kind of system had latched onto him, or why it sounded like it was barely holding itself together.

But as the chaos slowly stabilized, as the sensory flood reduced to something barely manageable, one thought rose above the rest—clear, familiar, unmistakably his.

No UI.

No HUD.

No pause button.

Just reality, pressing in on all sides.

Mike let go of the scream, letting it taper off into weak, hitching breaths. He didn’t fight the hands holding him. Didn’t resist the new rhythms his body demanded.

He observed.

He processed.

And somewhere, deep beneath the overload and confusion, the instinct to improve quietly took root once more.

## Scene IV — First Breath, First Data

The world did not slow down for him.

If anything, it became louder.

Light pressed in from every direction, leaking through eyelids that refused to stay shut. Even with his eyes closed, the brightness registered as a constant, dull ache. Sound layered itself on top—voices overlapping, sharp and excited, punctuated by sudden bursts of laughter or concern.

Mike drifted in and out of awareness.

Sleep came easily, but it wasn’t restful. Each time he surfaced, the same flood of sensation greeted him, relentless and unfiltered. There was no gradual acclimation, no easing-in period. His new body accepted input the way it accepted everything else: all at once, without negotiation.

When he woke again, something warm and soft was pressed against his cheek.

Fabric.

Not rough. Not cheap.

The texture registered slowly, detail resolving as his awareness sharpened. Fine weave. Clean. Recently laundered. He shifted instinctively, or tried to. The movement was clumsy, poorly coordinated, but the fabric adjusted, cradling him rather than resisting.

Held, then.

Good.

He breathed in.

The air smelled different here. Not the stale mix of electronics and city dust he remembered. This was… cleaner. Warm. Carried faint traces of something floral, something woody, and underneath it all, the sharp, mineral tang of polished stone.

Stone floors, he concluded.

Big space.

That thought anchored him.

He cracked his eyes open.

The world snapped into chaos.

Shapes loomed too close, edges blurring and doubling. Colors bled into one another, overwhelming in their saturation. Movement everywhere—hands, sleeves, faces leaning in and pulling back again.

Mike blinked rapidly, trying to force clarity. His eyes burned in protest, watering almost immediately.

Okay. Too much. Dial it back.

He let his eyelids droop, leaving only a thin slit of vision. The overload receded slightly, enough for patterns to emerge.

Ceiling.

High. Arched.

Not drywall.

The surface was smooth, pale, etched with subtle geometric patterns that caught the light. Decorative, not structural.

Wealth, he thought.

Not ostentatious. Controlled.

A voice rose above the others, firm but warm. The tone cut through the noise, drawing attention without force. The surrounding voices quieted in response.

Authority.

The words themselves were meaningless to him, a stream of unfamiliar sounds, but the cadence told him enough. Whoever was speaking was used to being obeyed.

He felt himself being shifted again, lifted more securely. Arms adjusted around him, careful and practiced.

The system stirred.

> **…Visual processing improving…**

Good.

He focused on that voice, letting the sound wash over him, mapping its rhythm. There were repeated phonemes, consistent structures. Language, definitely. Complex, but not alien.

Human, then.

That helped.

A new face entered his limited field of view, hovering closer than the others. Features blurred, but the proportions were right. Two eyes. Nose. Mouth. Skin tone warm, healthy.

The face looked at him intently.

Mike held the gaze.

The face stilled.

There was a subtle shift in the air, a pause that rippled outward. The surrounding voices hushed further, tension spiking.

Uh-oh, Mike thought faintly.

He hadn’t meant to stare. It was just… instinct. Track movement. Lock onto points of interest. Old habits didn’t disappear just because the hardware had changed.

The face smiled, slowly.

The tension broke.

Laughter followed, light and relieved. The voice spoke again, tone softened now, affectionate.

The system updated.

> **…Social response positive…**

Noted.

Mike relaxed, as much as his tiny body would allow. The effort cost him. His muscles trembled, then gave up, leaving him limp in the cradle of arms.

Exhaustion hit him hard, fast and absolute. His awareness dimmed, edges blurring.

Before he slipped away, something else caught his attention.

Movement at the periphery.

Figures standing back from the cluster, still and alert. They wore darker clothing, less decorative, their posture rigid. Hands rested near their waists, close to objects that felt important.

Guards.

Not ceremonial.

Professional.

Placement confirmed.

Sleep claimed him.

***

When he woke again, the world was quieter.

Not silent—never silent—but subdued. The lighting was softer now, indirect. Shadows stretched lazily across the room, suggesting a shift in time.

He lay on something firm but padded, wrapped in layers of fabric that retained warmth without suffocating. He wriggled slightly, testing the boundaries of his new body.

Limited range of motion. Poor strength. Response lag significant.

Annoying.

He stopped moving.

Observation first.

The room resolved gradually as his eyes adjusted. The ceiling again, closer this time. Walls lined with shelves and decorative panels. No visible screens. No exposed wiring. Everything felt… intentional.

Designed.

Footsteps approached, light and measured.

The same voice from before spoke, softer now, closer. Mike turned his head toward the sound, movement jerky but deliberate.

The voice paused.

He felt eyes on him.

He opened his mouth.

No words came out. Just a soft, indistinct sound.

The face leaned closer, smiling again. A hand brushed his forehead, gentle.

The system chimed faintly in the background of his awareness.

> **…Caretaker identified…**

Caretaker implied investment.

Resources.

Protection.

Mike let his eyes close, then opened them again slowly, as if struggling. The gesture felt… right. Weak, but responsive. Infant-appropriate.

The face brightened, emotion spiking.

Good.

He was already learning.

Time passed in fragments after that. Feeding. Being moved. Voices cycling in and out. Each interaction added to his growing internal model of the environment.

Patterns emerged quickly.

The caretakers were attentive, numerous. Response times were short. Any sign of distress brought immediate action.

High-value asset, then.

He cataloged faces, voices, tones. Not names—not yet—but roles. Authority. Care. Security. Logistics.

The system fed him occasional updates, each one clearer than the last.

> **…Language comprehension improving…**

> **…Cognitive growth within parameters…**

> **…Growth protocols pending…**

That last one lingered.

Pending meant future activation. A delay. A window.

Opportunity.

Mike didn’t know what kind of world he’d entered. Didn’t know its rules, its dangers, its ceiling.

But as he lay there, swaddled in comfort and guarded by people who clearly mattered, one conclusion crystallized with absolute certainty.

This wasn’t randomness.

This wasn’t luck.

This was placement.

And placement meant advantage.

His tiny fingers curled reflexively, grasping at the fabric near his chest. The motion was weak, uncoordinated—but intentional.

Not a fist.

A grasp.

The first data point in a long chain of decisions yet to come.

## Scene V — The First Silent Decision

Time did not move forward so much as it _accumulated_.

Mike experienced it in layers rather than lines. Wakefulness and sleep blended together, separated less by clear boundaries than by shifts in sensation—light dimming, sounds softening, the steady rhythm of breath and heartbeat becoming more pronounced.

The mechanical presence never left.

It lingered at the edge of his awareness, a constant, low-level hum that rose occasionally into something almost intelligible before slipping back into abstraction. Not a voice in the conventional sense. More like a system running diagnostics in the background, its outputs leaking into consciousness.

> **…Observe… Process… Improve…**

The sequence repeated often enough that it began to feel familiar.

Comforting, even.

Mike didn’t respond to it. Not because he couldn’t, but because there was nothing to respond _with_. No interface. No input channel. Just reception.

That was fine.

He listened.

He learned.

The nonsense gradually stopped being nonsense.

At first, it was just sound. Cadence without meaning. Repetition without structure. But Mike’s mind—compressed though it was—latched onto patterns with quiet efficiency. Certain sounds appeared together. Others followed predictable rhythms. Some were reserved for moments of heightened activity; others accompanied calm.

Language, assembling itself piece by piece.

By the time he could hold his eyes open for more than a few seconds at a time, the system’s transmissions had shifted. Less fragmented. More deliberate.

> **…Cognitive adaptation within expected parameters…**

Expected by whom? Mike wondered faintly.

The thought drifted by without urgency. He filed it away for later. Questions were cheap. Timing mattered more.

He focused instead on the people.

They came and went with regularity, their presence forming the backbone of his early environment. The primary caretaker—soft voice, steady hands—appeared most often. Others rotated in, each with distinct patterns of movement and sound.

He watched them all.

The system approved.

> **…Observation efficiency increasing…**

Mike noticed things they didn’t think to hide.

The way conversations shifted when certain figures entered the room. The subtle stiffening of posture. The lowering of voices. The difference in how quickly requests were obeyed.

Hierarchy.

He recognized it instantly.

Power wasn’t loud here. It was quiet, assumed. Built into the space, the routines, the way everyone moved without being told.

This family—his family, apparently—sat high within it.

That mattered.

He also noticed what _wasn’t_ present.

No shouting. No chaos. No desperation. Needs were met before they became urgent. Problems were addressed early, often before he was even aware of them.

A stable system.

Efficient.

Good.

The system murmured approval.

> **…Environmental stability high…**

> **…Resource availability: Abundant…**

Mike relaxed into it, letting his new body dictate its limited demands while his mind worked quietly in the background.

By the time his eyes could track movement reliably, he understood far more than anyone realized.

He still couldn’t speak. Couldn’t even control his expressions properly. His face did things without consulting him first—grimaces, slack-jawed stares, unfocused blinks.

It was frustrating.

But manageable.

He adjusted.

When caretakers approached, he let his gaze wander until the last moment, then focused suddenly, as if by accident. The effect was… interesting. People reacted to eye contact, even from an infant. They leaned in. Smiled. Engaged.

When voices grew tense, he fussed—just enough to redirect attention. Not a cry. A soft sound, easily soothed.

The tension dissolved.

> **…Behavioral modulation effective…**

Good.

He didn’t overuse it. That would attract notice. Patterns were powerful, but so was _absence_. He needed to be present without being remarkable.

Time continued to pile up.

Days, maybe weeks—hard to tell without reference points. His body changed subtly. Strength increased. Coordination improved, incrementally.

The system updated him in fragments.

> **…Motor control improving…**

> **…Neural plasticity elevated…**

The mechanical tone remained neutral, but Mike sensed something like anticipation beneath it. As if it were waiting for a trigger.

He was waiting too.

The first real test came unexpectedly.

He lay in his cradle, half-awake, the room bathed in low evening light. Voices drifted in from beyond his limited field of view—two of them, engaged in conversation.

Their tone was relaxed. Casual.

They weren’t talking _to_ him.

Which meant they weren’t filtering themselves.

Mike listened.

The language still wasn’t fully clear, but enough had snapped into place that meaning emerged from context. Names. Relationships. Titles. A mention of lineage, spoken with quiet pride.

And then—him.

Not his name. He didn’t have one yet, or at least not one he recognized. But the reference was unmistakable. A shift in tone. Warmth. Expectation.

Plans.

Mike processed that slowly.

Expectation was a double-edged thing.

High expectations brought resources, attention, opportunity.

They also brought scrutiny.

He didn’t want scrutiny.

Not yet.

The system pulsed, as if in agreement.

> **…Growth protocols pending…**

Pending meant _optional_.

It meant delay was possible.

Mike made his first true decision then—not a reflex, not an adaptation, but a deliberate choice.

He would not rush.

He would not signal more than necessary.

He would grow—but quietly.

The idea settled into place with surprising ease, as if it had been waiting for him to notice it.

He let his breathing even out. Let his awareness fade just enough to mimic sleep. The voices continued for a bit longer, then moved away.

The room fell quiet again.

Mike remained awake.

His tiny fingers flexed, then stilled. He felt the weight of the blankets, the warmth of the air, the steady presence of guards beyond the walls.

Safe.

Placed.

Given time.

The system hummed softly, its directives aligning with his own intent.

> **…Observe… Process… Improve…**

Mike didn’t need to be told anymore.

He observed.

He processed.

And, in the privacy of his own compressed awareness, he planned.

Not conquest.

Not domination.

Just survival—optimized.

Growth—controlled.

Attention—minimized.

For now.

His fingers curled again, brushing against the fabric beneath him.

A small movement. Insignificant.

But it wasn’t a fist.

It was a grasp.

And this time, he knew exactly what he was reaching for.
